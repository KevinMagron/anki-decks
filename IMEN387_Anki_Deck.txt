What is the core difference between a real asset and a financial asset?	A <b>real asset</b> helps produce goods/services or has direct productive use (factories, machines, land, software/IP). A <b>financial asset</b> is a claim on future cash flows generated by real assets, firms, households, or governments.	IMEN387::Lecture 1	lecture_1 theory foundations
Why do financial assets exist if real assets are what actually produce value?	They let savings and risk be transferred between people. Investors can fund productive projects without directly owning/operating the underlying real assets.	IMEN387::Lecture 1	lecture_1 discussion foundations
What is a financial instrument?	A contract that creates a financial asset for one party and usually a financial liability or equity claim for another. Major categories include debt, equity, and derivatives.	IMEN387::Lecture 1	lecture_1 theory instruments
What is the difference between debt and equity?	<b>Debt</b>: contractual payments are promised and creditors have priority. <b>Equity</b>: residual ownership; shareholders receive what remains after obligations are paid.	IMEN387::Lecture 1	lecture_1 theory instruments
What is a derivative?	A contract whose payoff/value depends on an underlying asset, rate, index, price, or other variable.	IMEN387::Lecture 1	lecture_1 theory derivatives Hull_Ch1
Money market vs capital market: what is the underlying distinction?	<b>Money markets</b> are mainly short-term funding markets. <b>Capital markets</b> are mainly longer-term debt and equity markets.	IMEN387::Lecture 1	lecture_1 theory markets
What is a U.S. Treasury bill (T-bill)?	A short-term U.S. government security with no coupon. It is typically bought below face value and pays face value at maturity.	IMEN387::Lecture 1	lecture_1 theory treasury
What does it mean that a T-bill is sold at a discount?	You pay less than face value today and receive face value later, e.g. pay $98 today and receive $100 at maturity. The difference is your return.	IMEN387::Lecture 1	lecture_1 theory treasury
T-bill vs Treasury note/bond: what is the main structural difference?	T-bills are short-term and normally have no coupons. Treasury notes/bonds have longer maturities and usually pay periodic coupons.	IMEN387::Lecture 1	lecture_1 theory treasury
Primary market vs secondary market?	<b>Primary market</b>: the issuer sells newly created securities and receives the funds. <b>Secondary market</b>: investors trade existing securities with each other.	IMEN387::Lecture 1	lecture_1 theory markets
What is the economic purpose of a secondary market?	Liquidity and price discovery. Investors can exit positions without forcing the issuer to repay them immediately.	IMEN387::Lecture 1	lecture_1 discussion markets
What is a forward contract in one sentence?	A customized OTC agreement made today to buy/sell an underlying at a specified future date for a specified price.	IMEN387::Lecture 1	lecture_1 theory derivatives Hull_Ch1
What is the single underlying distinction between forwards and futures?	A forward is typically customized OTC and mainly settled at maturity; a future is standardized, exchange-traded, margined, and marked to market daily.	IMEN387::Lecture 1	lecture_1 compare derivatives Hull_Ch2
What is the single underlying distinction between an option and a forward/future?	A forward/future creates an <b>obligation</b>. An option gives the buyer a <b>right without an obligation</b> to exercise.	IMEN387::Lecture 1	lecture_1 compare derivatives Hull_Ch1 Hull_Ch10
What is a call option? What is a put option?	<b>Call</b>: right to buy the underlying at the strike. <b>Put</b>: right to sell the underlying at the strike.	IMEN387::Lecture 1	lecture_1 theory options Hull_Ch10
What do long and short mean?	<b>Long</b> means you own/buy the position and generally benefit when its payoff rises. <b>Short</b> means you sold/took the opposite side and generally benefit when the long side loses.	IMEN387::Lecture 1	lecture_1 theory markets
Why can a financial instrument be valuable even if it does not create a new real asset?	It can reallocate financing, risk, timing of cash flows, and ownership. That can make investment and production easier even though the instrument itself is only a claim.	IMEN387::Lecture 1	lecture_1 discussion foundations
What are the two broad financial decisions of a corporation?	<b>Investment decision</b>: which real projects/assets should the firm invest in? <b>Financing decision</b>: how should those projects be funded (debt, equity, retained earnings, etc.)?	IMEN387::Lecture 2	lecture_2 theory corporate_finance

What does a competitive financial market imply about obvious profit opportunities?	If an opportunity is easy to identify and exploit, many traders act on it quickly, changing prices and reducing or eliminating the opportunity.	IMEN387::Lecture 2	lecture_2 theory market_efficiency

Active vs passive investing: what is the underlying distinction?	<b>Active</b> tries to outperform a benchmark by selecting/timing securities. <b>Passive</b> mainly accepts market prices and tracks a benchmark with low turnover/cost.	IMEN387::Lecture 2	lecture_2 compare investing

During a banking crisis, why can an interbank benchmark rise relative to T-bill yields?	Bank credit/liquidity risk rises, so lenders demand more compensation to lend to banks. Defaults → losses and uncertainty about bank balance sheets → lower trust/creditworthiness → banks hoard liquidity or demand higher interbank rates → funding spreads widen → lending can contract. What is a 'flight to quality'?	Investors move from risky/uncertain assets into perceived safe and liquid assets such as government securities during stress.	IMEN387::Lecture 2	lecture_2 theory financial_crisis

Through counterparty chains, shared asset fire sales, collateral calls, funding markets, payment systems, and confidence effects. This is why financial risk can become systemic.	IMEN387::Lecture 2	lecture_2 discussion financial_crisis

Why can very fast information processing make markets look 'efficient' without making them perfectly correct?	Prices can incorporate information quickly but investors can still misinterpret information, face model uncertainty, or disagree. Why are common shareholders called residual claimants?	They receive what remains after contractual obligations and higher-priority claims have been paid.	IMEN387::Lecture 3	lecture_3 theory capital_structure

What is the weak-form Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)?	Current prices already reflect information contained in past prices and trading data. Pure technical patterns should not reliably generate abnormal risk-adjusted returns.	IMEN387::Lecture 3	lecture_3 theory EMH

What is the strong-form EMH?	Prices reflect all information, public and private. This is the strongest and least realistic version.	IMEN387::Lecture 3	lecture_3 theory EMH
Does EMH mean prices always equal true intrinsic value?	No. It mainly says exploitable mispricing based on the relevant information set is difficult to earn from consistently. Prices can still be wrong or change when beliefs/information change.	IMEN387::Lecture 3	lecture_3 discussion EMH

How can a derivative transfer risk without eliminating risk from the system?	One party hedges by giving up/transferring exposure; another party voluntarily takes the opposite exposure. The risk is reallocated rather than magically destroyed.	IMEN387::Lecture 3	lecture_3 discussion derivatives

What is credit risk?	The risk that a borrower/counterparty does not make promised payments fully and on time.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 theory credit_risk

Instrument vs benchmark rate: T-bill vs SOFR?	A T-bill is an actual security you can buy. SOFR is a reference rate/benchmark calculated from eligible overnight Treasury repo transactions.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 compare rates

Annual compounding formula for principal \(A\), annual rate \(r\), and \(T\) years?	\(FV=A(1+r)^T\).	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 math compounding
If a nominal annual rate \(r\) is compounded \(n\) times per year, why does \(r/n\) appear?	The quoted annual nominal rate is split across \(n\) compounding periods, so each period receives rate \(r/n\), and there are \(nT\) periods.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 derive compounding

How does continuous compounding arise from discrete compounding?	As \(n\to\infty\), \(\left(1+\frac{r}{n}\right)^{nT}\to e^{rT}\), so \(FV=Ae^{rT}\).	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 derive compounding
Continuous-compounding future value and present value formulas?	\(FV=PV\,e^{rT}\) and \(PV=FV\,e^{-rT}\). Moving backward in time divides by the growth factor.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 math discounting
What is a discount factor under continuous compounding?	For cash flow at time \(T\), the discount factor is \(e^{-rT}\). Multiply a future cash flow by it to convert it to today's value.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 theory discounting
What is a T-year zero rate (spot rate)?	The annualized rate for an investment with one payoff at time \(T\) and no intermediate coupons.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 theory zero_rates Hull_Ch4
Why can a zero-coupon investment still use compounding even though it pays nothing before maturity?	Compounding describes how value accumulates mathematically, not whether cash is physically distributed along the way.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 debug zero_rates
What is a Treasury zero rate?	A zero-coupon/spot rate inferred from U.S. Treasury prices for a particular maturity. It is often used as a low-credit-risk discount benchmark.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 theory zero_rates treasury
What is the zero curve / term structure of zero rates?	The collection of zero rates \(R(T)\) across maturities \(T\). It tells you the market discount rate for single cash flows at different future dates.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 theory zero_curve
Why is 'longer maturity → higher interest rate' not a law?	The yield curve reflects expected future short rates, inflation, risk/liquidity premia, and supply/demand. It can be upward sloping, flat, inverted, or humped.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 discussion term_structure
How do you price a coupon bond using zero rates?	Discount each cash flow using the zero rate for <b>that cash flow's maturity</b>, then add: \(B=\sum_i CF_i e^{-R_i t_i}\) under continuous compounding.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 derive bond_pricing Hull_Ch4
Why not discount every coupon of a 2-year bond using only the 2-year zero rate?	Because the coupons arrive at different dates. A cash flow received in 6 months should be valued with a 6-month discount factor, not as if you wait 2 years.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 discussion bond_pricing
Coupon rate vs coupon payment?	Coupon rate is the annual percentage of face value specified by the bond. Coupon payment is the cash amount. Example: 6% coupon on $100 face, paid semiannually → $3 every 6 months.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 compare bonds
Coupon rate vs bond yield?	Coupon rate determines contractual cash payments. Bond yield is the market-implied return/discount rate consistent with the bond's current price and cash flows.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 compare bond_yield
What is yield to maturity (YTM) conceptually?	The single discount rate \(y\) that makes the present value of all promised bond cash flows equal the observed bond price, under the chosen compounding convention.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 theory bond_yield Hull_Ch4
Can a coupon bond's YTM usually be isolated with a simple closed-form formula?	No. With multiple coupon periods, \(y\) generally appears in several powers/exponents, so YTM is usually solved numerically.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 discussion bond_yield
What is par yield?	For a given maturity, the coupon rate that a newly issued bond would need so its price equals face value (par), given today's zero curve.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 theory par_yield Hull_Ch4
If an existing bond's coupon rate is below the current par yield for its maturity, where should it tend to trade?	Below par, because its coupons are less attractive than what a new par bond would pay at current market rates.	IMEN387::Lecture 5	lecture_5 discussion par_yield
What is bootstrapping a zero curve?	A sequential method: use the shortest-maturity instrument to solve the shortest zero rate, then use that known rate to strip earlier cash flows from the next bond and solve the next zero rate, and continue outward.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 theory bootstrap Hull_Ch4
Why does bootstrapping work sequentially?	A longer coupon bond contains cash flows at earlier dates plus one new later date. If earlier discount factors are already known, the only unknown is the new later discount factor/rate.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion bootstrap
Bootstrap derivation: a 1-year zero-coupon bond pays 100 and costs 95. What is the 1-year continuously compounded zero rate?	Use \(95=100e^{-R_1(1)}\). Thus \(R_1=-\ln(95/100)\approx5.13\%\).	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 derivation bootstrap
Bootstrap derivation: a 2-year annual-coupon bond has price \(P\), coupon \(C\), face \(F\), and known 1-year zero rate \(R_1\). Write the equation that solves \(R_2\).	\(P=Ce^{-R_1}+(C+F)e^{-2R_2}\). Rearrange: \(e^{-2R_2}=\frac{P-Ce^{-R_1}}{C+F}\), so \(R_2=-\frac12\ln\!\left(\frac{P-Ce^{-R_1}}{C+F}\right)\).	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 derivation bootstrap
What is a forward interest rate?	The interest rate implied today for borrowing/lending during a future time interval, consistent with today's zero curve and no-arbitrage.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 theory forward_rates Hull_Ch4
Derive the continuously compounded forward rate from \(T_1\) to \(T_2\) using zero rates \(R_1,R_2\).	No-arbitrage requires \(e^{R_2T_2}=e^{R_1T_1}e^{f(T_2-T_1)}\). Taking logs gives \(f=\frac{R_2T_2-R_1T_1}{T_2-T_1}\).	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 derivation forward_rates
Economic intuition for the forward-rate formula?	Investing until \(T_2\) directly must match investing until \(T_1\) and then rolling into the implied future rate; otherwise there would be an arbitrage.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion forward_rates
Does a forward rate equal the market's certain forecast of the future spot rate?	No. It is a no-arbitrage rate implied by today's curve. It may reflect expectations plus risk/liquidity premia, depending on the market/model.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion forward_rates
What is a Forward Rate Agreement (FRA)?	An OTC contract that locks an interest rate today for a specified notional principal over a future borrowing/lending period.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 theory FRA Hull_Ch4
What risk does an FRA hedge?	Future interest-rate uncertainty. A borrower can lock a future borrowing rate; a lender can lock a future lending rate.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion FRA
What is the basic economic payoff idea of an FRA?	At settlement, the parties exchange the economic difference between the agreed fixed rate and the market reference rate for the future period, applied to the notional and accrual fraction.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 theory FRA
What is an interest-rate swap?	A contract where two parties exchange interest-payment streams on a notional principal, commonly fixed-rate payments for floating-rate payments.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 theory swaps Hull_Ch7
Why might a company enter a fixed-for-floating interest-rate swap?	To transform its interest-rate exposure: e.g. convert floating-rate debt into economically fixed-rate debt, or vice versa, without refinancing the original loan.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion swaps
What is the conceptual link between a plain-vanilla interest-rate swap and bonds?	A swap can be viewed as the difference between a fixed-rate bond and a floating-rate bond, which helps with valuation.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion swaps Hull_Ch7
What is bond duration in the continuous-compounding framework used in your slide?	\(D=\frac{\sum_i t_i\,CF_i e^{-yt_i}}{B}\). It is the present-value-weighted average time of the bond's cash flows and gives first-order yield sensitivity.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 theory duration Hull_Ch4
Why do we use present-value weights rather than simply averaging the cash-flow dates?	A large $1,000 payment should matter far more than a small $10 coupon. PV weights measure how much of the bond's current economic value is located at each date.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion duration
"What does \(t_i	imes PV(CF_i)\) mean intuitively?"	It is the contribution of cash flow \(i\) to the bond's 'center of gravity in time': amount of current value × how far in the future it sits.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion duration

What is modified duration?	If yield is compounded \(m\) times/year, \(D^*=\frac{D}{1+y/m}\). It is the duration measure directly used for approximate percentage price sensitivity. What is dollar duration?	\(D^{**}=D^*B\). It converts percentage sensitivity into currency sensitivity: \(\Delta B\approx-D^{**}\Delta y\).	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 theory dollar_duration

Why is a rise in market yields bad for the market value of a bond you already own?	Your bond's fixed payments are now less attractive than newly available higher-rate investments, so its market price must fall.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion duration
Why can higher rates still have a benefit for a long-term bond investor?	Coupons and future cash can be reinvested at higher rates. Rate changes create both price risk and reinvestment effects.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion duration

Asset-liability duration matching: what are we trying to cancel?	We want small parallel rate changes to produce approximately equal dollar changes in assets and liabilities, so net economic value changes little.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion immunization

If you own a bond and its price falls, you lose. If you issued that bond as a liability and its market value falls, what happens economically? What assumption is hidden in the simple portfolio-duration relationship?	A <b>small parallel shift</b> in the yield curve: yields at all relevant maturities move by approximately the same amount.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 theory duration_limits
Why does duration become inaccurate for large yield changes?	The bond price–yield relationship is curved, while duration is only the local linear (slope) approximation.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion convexity
What is convexity intuitively?	A measure of the curvature of the bond price–yield relationship: it tells how the slope/sensitivity itself changes as yield changes.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 theory convexity Hull_Ch4
Duration vs convexity in one sentence?	Duration is the local <b>slope</b>; convexity is the <b>bend/curvature</b>.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 compare convexity
Convexity formula under continuously compounded yield?	\(C=\frac{1}{B}\frac{\partial^2B}{\partial y^2}=\frac{\sum_i CF_i t_i^2e^{-yt_i}}{B}\).	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 math convexity
Why does \(t_i^2\) appear in convexity?	Convexity comes from the second derivative with respect to yield. Differentiating \(e^{-yt_i}\) twice brings down \(t_i^2\). Economically, far-dated cash flows contribute disproportionately to curvature.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 derive convexity
What is the duration-plus-convexity approximation?	\(\frac{\Delta B}{B}\approx-D\Delta y+\frac12C(\Delta y)^2\). The second term corrects the linear duration estimate for curvature.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 math convexity
Why is convexity much more important for large yield changes than small ones?	Because its correction contains \((\Delta y)^2\). Squaring a tiny yield move makes the term very small; as the move grows, the convexity term grows quickly.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion convexity
Two portfolios have the same duration. Why can they react differently to a large rate move?	They can have different convexity. Same duration means same local slope today, not the same curvature away from today's yield.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion convexity
At the same duration, why can payments spread over a long period create more convexity than payments concentrated near one date?	Convexity weights timing approximately with \(t^2\), so very early and very late cash flows create more second-order curvature than cash flows concentrated around the average time.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion convexity
Why is positive convexity generally desirable, holding duration and price comparable?	For the same magnitude rate move, more positive convexity means more upside when yields fall and less downside when yields rise than the straight duration estimate would imply.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion convexity
What risk remains even after matching duration and convexity?	Nonparallel yield-curve moves (steepening, flattening, twists), plus credit spread changes, liquidity changes, optionality, and other risks.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion risk_management
Bond yield derivation card: how do you define the YTM mathematically for annual coupon \(C\), face \(F\), maturity \(n\), price \(B\), with annual compounding?	Find \(y\) such that \(B=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\frac{C}{(1+y)^t}+\frac{C+F}{(1+y)^n}\). The YTM is the root of this equation.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 derivation bond_yield
Why is a single YTM less fundamental than the zero curve for pricing?	YTM compresses all cash flows into one average internal rate. The zero curve provides a separate discount rate for each maturity and therefore prices each cash flow directly.	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 discussion bond_yield zero_curve

Thus \(C=\frac{100(1-d_n)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_i}\). The annual par yield is \(C/100\).	IMEN387::Lecture 6	lecture_6 derivation par_yield
What is the core mathematical pattern behind bond pricing, bootstrapping, forward rates, duration, and convexity?	Start from discounted cash flows. <b>Pricing</b> sums them; <b>bootstrapping</b> solves unknown discount factors; <b>forward rates</b> enforce no-arbitrage across dates; <b>duration/convexity</b> differentiate price with respect to yield.
